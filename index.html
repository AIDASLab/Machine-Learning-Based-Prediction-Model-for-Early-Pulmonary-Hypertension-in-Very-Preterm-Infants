Abstract

Early pulmonary hypertension affects up to 30–55% of infants born before 28 weeks and contributes to bronchopulmonary dysplasia, right heart failure, and death. It is difficult to catch in time: echocardiography is intermittent and operator-dependent, evidence for biomarker-based screening is limited, and risk factors have largely been studied one at a time.

We trained seven machine-learning model families on 57 routinely collected variables from the first 72 hours of life, in a cohort of 1,016 very preterm infants of whom 59 (5.8%) developed Early PH. All models exceeded AUROC 0.90; XGBoost reached AUROC 0.9527, identifying three of every four cases in the held-out test set while flagging 2.6% of unaffected infants. SHAP analysis attributed the largest contributions to markers of early hemodynamic instability and of oxygen and ventilatory requirement.

Methods

We reviewed 1,259 very preterm or very-low-birth-weight infants admitted to the Seoul National University Children’s Hospital NICU between 2013 and 2025. Early PH was defined as echocardiographically confirmed pulmonary hypertension with treatment started on postnatal days 4–28. After exclusions, 1,016 inborn infants remained, 59 of them with Early PH — 5.8%. That imbalance is why the results below report precision and PR-AUC alongside AUROC.

The model uses 57 variables, every one of them closing at 72 hours of life. Postnatal day 4 begins at 72 hours, so the predictor window ends exactly where the outcome window starts: no feature is measured after an infant could first meet the Early PH definition. All 57 are recorded in routine care, so the model needs no measurement that is not already being taken.

Results

Seven model families — nine configurations, counting the imputation variants — were trained on the same features and the same stratified 80:20 split. All exceeded AUROC 0.90. XGBoost was the strongest at 0.9527, identifying three of every four Early PH cases (recall 0.750) while flagging 2.6% of unaffected infants (specificity 0.974).

The models differ far more in where their operating points sit than in AUROC. Random Forest almost never flags a healthy infant but finds only one case in six; ElasticNet and SVM catch nearly every case at the cost of many false alarms. XGBoost has the most balanced operating point of the nine. These are the thresholds used in the study, not thresholds tuned for deployment.

Why PR-AUC matters here. AUROC was the study’s primary metric, but Early PH occurred in only 5.8% of the cohort. At that prevalence a random classifier scores a PR-AUC of about 0.058, so the observed 0.55–0.67 is the figure that shows what the models add under the imbalance they will actually meet.

Analysis

SHAP attributes each prediction to individual features, which allows the model’s inputs to be compared against what is already known about neonatal physiology. Four directions were consistent:

  • Hypotension increased predicted Early PH probability.
  • Hydrocortisone treatment for hypotension increased it.
  • A higher SpO2/FiO2 ratio lowered it — poorer oxygenation efficiency pushed predictions up.
  • Greater ventilatory burden increased it.

Together these point to infants with poor early hemodynamic stability and a high oxygen and ventilatory requirement, which is consistent with what is known about the condition. Several of the strongest contributors — hydrocortisone, PDA treatment, escalating support — are interventions given because an infant is unstable, so the model may be reflecting illness severity and its management as much as pulmonary hypertension specifically. These are associations within the model, not causes.
